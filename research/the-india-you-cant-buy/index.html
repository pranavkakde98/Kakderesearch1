The India You Can’t Buy

Why the index a foreign allocator can own is not the economy they think they are buying.

By Pranav Kakde 2026 28 pages, about 9 minutes online

Executive summary

  • A foreign tracker owns the float foreigners are allowed to hold, not the economy.
  • Foreign ownership limits, free-float adjustment and promoter holdings mean index weight understates economic weight in the most domestically-owned sectors.
  • The gap is structural, not a discount that closes on its own.
  • The practical question for an allocator is not whether to hold India, but whether the exposure held matches the exposure intended.

The question

For most foreign allocators, India exposure arrives through an index. A committee approves an allocation, a tracker or a benchmarked mandate is selected, and the position appears in the book labelled India. The label does a lot of work. This paper asks a narrow question about it: how closely does the India an allocator can buy through the index correspond to the India in the growth argument that justified the allocation?

The growth argument itself is not in dispute here. On the IMF’s projections, India’s real GDP growth over 2026-30 averages 6.5 percent, against 3.9 for China, 2.0 for the United States and 1.2 for the euro area.

An allocator who wants that economy in the portfolio is not being eccentric. The difficulty sits in the word wants. What the index delivers is not the economy. It is the part of the economy that is listed, floating and legally available to foreigners, and those three filters are not neutral.

What an index can actually hold

An investable index is built backwards from what its user is permitted to own. Three mechanisms do the filtering.

The first is listing. An index can only contain listed companies, and every economy holds a large share of its activity outside the listed market. In India that unlisted share includes significant state-owned enterprise and a deep layer of family-held business. The listed universe is a sample of the economy, not a miniature of it.

The second is free float. Index providers scale a company’s weight to the shares actually available to trade. Holdings that never move, promoter stakes in the founder-controlled groups that anchor much of Indian corporate ownership, and government stakes in public-sector enterprises, are excluded from the calculation. A company can be large in the economy and small in the index because most of it does not trade.

The third filter applies to foreign investors specifically. Indian regulation caps foreign ownership in a range of sectors, and index providers adjust further for foreign room, the portion of the permitted foreign quota not already taken up. Where foreign investors have used most of their allowance in a stock, the weight that new foreign money can be directed towards is reduced accordingly.

Each adjustment is defensible on its own terms. An index that ignored them would direct money into shares that cannot be bought. But the adjustments compound, and the compounded result is an index that tilts systematically away from the parts of the economy that are most domestically owned, and towards the companies whose ownership is most open to the world.

Where the gap is widest

The tilt is not evenly spread. It is deepest exactly where domestic ownership is deepest.

Public-sector enterprises carry government majority stakes, which shrink their float and therefore their index presence relative to their weight in the economy. Banking, the sector through which India’s credit cycle actually runs, operates under its own foreign ownership caps, so the exposure a foreign tracker has to Indian banking is a constrained version of the real thing. Across the industrial economy, promoter-controlled groups keep a large share of their equity permanently off the market, which holds down their float-adjusted weight however large their operations grow.

Set against that, the companies weighted most heavily in the index are those most available to foreign capital. That is not a criticism of those companies. It is an observation about what the index optimises for: availability, not representativeness. An allocator reading a factsheet sees sector weights. What the factsheet does not say is that those weights answer the question of what foreigners can buy, not the question of what the Indian economy is made of.

What this means for an allocation decision

None of this is an argument against holding index exposure to India. For most foreign allocators it remains the cheapest and cleanest instrument available. The argument is about what the position should be understood to be.

An index position is a claim on the listed, floating, foreign-available slice of the economy. If the committee’s intention is exposure to that slice, the instrument fits the intention. If the intention is exposure to India’s growth in some broader sense, to credit expansion, to state-led infrastructure, to the domestically owned industrial base, then the instrument tracks the intention loosely, and the looseness is structural rather than a pricing anomaly that patience will fix. The gap does not close on its own because it is not a discount. It is a difference in the thing being held.

That distinction should show up in three places: in how the position is sized, in which benchmark the committee measures it against, and in what the committee expects the position to do when the domestic economy and the listed market diverge.

What would change our view

The framework stands or falls on ownership structure, so the honest list of what would weaken it is short and specific. A sustained relaxation of sectoral foreign ownership limits would narrow the gap directly. A broad, persistent decline in promoter holdings would raise free float and pull the index closer to the economy. A deep pipeline of listings from the state-owned and family-held economy would widen the sample the index is drawn from. Each of these is observable, and the full paper sets out the indicators we track for each one. None of them currently moves fast. If they begin to, this conclusion is the first thing we would revisit.
