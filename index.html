The principle

Underneath every emerging-market conclusion sits another question: compared with what?

An allocator weighing India is rarely weighing India alone. The same capital could go to China, Indonesia, Vietnam, Brazil, Mexico, Taiwan, Korea or a Gulf market; it could sit in the broad EM index; or it could never leave developed markets at all. The comparison set is chosen, not inherited — and choosing it correctly is the first analytical act, not a footnote.

Most country coverage skips that step. It is written to sell a market, or assembled from the same secondary sources the reader has already seen. Neither survives contact with an investment committee.

A country conclusion that cannot survive comparison with the markets competing for the same capital is not yet an investment conclusion.

    The dollar, US rates, oil and commodities, trade and geopolitics — not covered for their own sake, but through the channel by which they reach emerging-market earnings, currencies and valuations.

A growth rate is not a story. The same 7 per cent means one thing coming out of a contraction and something else entirely at the end of a long expansion.

2008 is the cleanest test of that. India kept growing through the global financial crisis, at 3.1 per cent, and the number that mattered was not the level but what it was measured against.

2020 took the series negative for the first time in four decades, to −5.8 per cent. The 9.7 per cent that followed in 2021 is arithmetic on a collapsed base, not an acceleration.

Which is why no market is read on its own here. Set against China, the United States and the euro area, the same line reads differently — and against the emerging markets competing for the same capital, differently again. That comparison is where every engagement starts.

Emerging markets strategy

Which market deserves the marginal dollar?

No emerging market exists in a portfolio vacuum. Comparative work across the economies competing for the same allocation — measured on the mechanisms that turn growth into currency-adjusted shareholder return, not on the country narrative.

  1. 01

    Country allocation

    Which market deserves the marginal dollar, and on what evidence. Relative growth, valuation, currency and investability, read as one decision rather than seven country notes.

  2. 02

    Cross-market valuation

    Price against earnings, ROE, currency and growth — which premiums the fundamentals actually support, and which are a story the market has agreed to keep telling.

  3. 03

    Capital flows & FX

    Foreign and domestic capital, the dollar cycle, rates and currencies — who is setting the price at the margin, and what a turn in the dollar changes across the set.

  4. 04

    Trade & manufacturing

    China+1, supply chains and export share — and the harder question of who captures the value: the host economy, the multinational, or the local shareholder.

  5. 05

    Market structure & investability

    Free float, ownership, governance, index construction and foreign access — the gap between the economy and the slice of it a foreigner is allowed to own.

The India You Can’t Buy

Why the index a foreign allocator can own is not the economy they think they are buying: foreign room, ownership limits, and the gap between MSCI India and India — the same investability question that decides where an emerging-market dollar can actually go.

  • A foreign tracker owns the float foreigners are allowed to hold, not the economy.
  • Ownership limits, free-float adjustment and promoter holdings mean index weight understates economic weight in the most domestically-owned sectors.
  • The gap is structural, not a discount that closes on its own.
  • The paper closes on what would change the view, stated as observable indicators.

    How the research is done

    The same six stages, every engagement

    1. 01

      Define the question

      Most enquiries arrive as a topic, and a topic cannot be answered. The scoping call turns it into something specific enough to be wrong about. If that cannot be done, the engagement does not start.

    2. 02

      Choose the comparison set

      The markets competing for the same capital are named before any analysis begins. A manufacturing question runs against Vietnam and Mexico; a domestic-demand question against Indonesia and Brazil. Choosing this set correctly is the first analytical act.

    3. 03

      Establish the evidence

      Official data, primary sources, policy documents and filings, pulled and cleaned in-house. Where a series has been revised or interrupted, that is recorded rather than smoothed over. Those breaks are usually where the interesting answers sit.

    4. 04

      Challenge the consensus

      The prevailing view is written down explicitly, along with what it rests on. Often that is a single dataset, a definitional choice, or a comparison nobody has revisited in years.

    5. 05

      Test the conclusion

      The finding is attacked before it is delivered. What alternative explanations produce the same result? Anything that only holds under one reading of the data is labelled as such.

    6. 06

      Make it decision-relevant

      The report closes on what you should now understand differently, and the conditions that would change the view. A conclusion you cannot defend when challenged has not finished being researched.

    The comparison set is chosen, not inherited.

    A good analysis does not reach for China and the United States by reflex. A manufacturing question runs against Vietnam, Mexico, Indonesia and China. A domestic-demand question runs against Indonesia and Brazil. A market-access question runs against the whole classified EM set. The question determines the peer group — and the first task of any engagement is getting that choice right.

    A researcher, not a research department.

    Before starting Kakde Research I worked in London, at a global investment bank and at a financial-data company, across macro and market data. I trained as a computer engineer before taking an MSc in Finance, and I still build the data pipelines behind every piece of work myself.

    The practice exists because of a gap I kept seeing from the other side. The emerging-market questions asked in London — and the India questions most of all — were rarely answered well from London: the coverage was either promotional, written to sell the market, or generic, written by someone reading the same secondary sources as the client. What was missing was a local reading with the discipline of an institutional desk.

    Every engagement is scoped, researched and delivered by the analyst you speak to. There is no account-manager handoff and no junior team separated from your original question, which places a limit on how much work the practice takes at once — and the limit is deliberate.
