What consulting is here

Research applied to one decision.

An engagement here is a research project with a decision at the end of it. It is not a transformation programme, a framework taken off the shelf or a team of associates billed by the week; it is not execution support, and it is not personalised securities advice. One analyst takes one question — which market, whether to enter, where the supply chain should sit, what a policy turn does to the plan — and researches it to institutional standard, against the alternatives that compete for the same capital, the same plant or the same customer.

Emerging markets are the opportunity set. India is our deepest specialist market, read from India continuously, so an India question arrives with the depth already built and an emerging-markets question arrives with India properly weighed inside it. The analyst who scopes the project is the analyst who does the work and presents it. There is no handoff, and that caps how many projects run at once — deliberately.

Seven engagement types

01–07 · Scoped to the decision

These are the shapes the work most often takes. They are the vocabulary of an engagement rather than a menu: a project is scoped to the decision in front of you and may draw on more than one of them. Each is delivered in the format the decision needs, agreed in writing before work starts.

01

Emerging-Market Opportunity Assessment

Which emerging markets deserve deeper attention for this objective — and which do not?

What it answers

A structured screen of the emerging-market universe against a stated objective — a new allocation, a manufacturing footprint, a sales presence, a growth theme — that names the markets worth the next stage of work and says plainly why the others are not. The result is a shortlist with reasons, not a ranking of everything.

The analytical work

Define the objective and the criteria it actually implies. Screen the candidate universe on official data: growth and demand, external accounts, credit, currency, market access, policy direction and sector fit. Test the shortlist against the objective’s real constraints — size, timing, ownership limits, repatriation — and record why each excluded market fell out.

Possible outputs

  • Research report
  • Executive memo
  • Committee presentation
  • Data appendix

Where it lands

A shortlist you can defend to a committee, a board or a partner, with the excluded markets accounted for and the next stage of work already scoped.

Typically commissioned by an allocation team, a head of strategy or a board deciding where to look next, before anyone commits time or capital to a single market.

02

Country & Market Comparison

Of the candidate markets, which one — measured like for like, on one framework?

What it answers

A like-for-like comparison of two or more named markets on a common framework built for the decision: growth and demand, prices and rates, external position, credit depth, currency regime, valuation, market access, governance and policy trajectory. The weightings are stated, and the verdict is argued rather than averaged.

The analytical work

Agree the candidate markets and the framework. Assemble the series from official and primary sources, cleaned in-house and dated. Normalise where definitions differ and say where they cannot be reconciled. Score, stress the ranking against alternative weightings, and write the case for and against each market so that any disagreement sits in the weights, not the facts.

Possible outputs

  • Research report
  • Strategy deck
  • Comparison dashboard
  • Data appendix

Where it lands

One document the whole team argues from, and a framework that can be re-run when the data or the objective changes.

Typically commissioned by an asset manager or family office choosing between markets for a fresh commitment, or a corporate strategy team deciding where a regional plan should anchor.

03

Market Entry & Expansion Strategy

Should you enter or expand — where, by which route, and on what evidence rather than on the pitch?

What it answers

For a company or investor weighing a specific entry or expansion: how large the addressable market is and how fast it is growing, how demand and channels are structured, who competes and how they win, what regulation, ownership and tax rules shape the route in, and which route — organic, partnership, acquisition, greenfield — fits the objective and the appetite. The comparison markets are named at the outset so the case is made against them, not in isolation.

The analytical work

Market sizing from official statistics and company disclosures rather than vendor totals. Demand and channel structure. Competitor mapping and economics. Regulatory and ownership review. Entry-route options with the trade-offs made explicit, and a risk register grounded in evidence rather than generic caution.

Possible outputs

  • Strategy deck
  • Research report
  • Financial model
  • Live workshop

Where it lands

An entry or expansion case a board or investment committee can approve, defer or decline with the reasons on the table — and a clear list of what remains to be settled.

Typically commissioned by a corporate strategy team, a business-unit head or a growth investor backing an operating company’s move into a new market.

04

China+1 & Supply-Chain Strategy

Where should the next plant, supplier or tier of the chain sit — for this sector, not in general?

What it answers

A sector-specific test of the China+1 thesis: which alternative markets — India, Vietnam, Indonesia, Mexico, Thailand or others, depending on the sector — can actually take the volume, at what cost and lead time, with what supplier depth, logistics, energy, labour, incentives and trade-agreement access; and where value would be captured locally rather than assembled and re-exported. The answer differs by sector, and the work says so.

The analytical work

Map the current chain and the exposure inside it. Define the candidate markets for the sector. Assemble trade, production, cost, tariff and incentive data; assess supplier ecosystems and infrastructure; model the landed-cost and lead-time trade-offs. Test the thesis against the alternatives — including the alternative of staying put.

Possible outputs

  • Research report
  • Landed-cost model
  • Committee presentation
  • Live workshop

Where it lands

A relocation or diversification decision that has been tested rather than presumed, with the exposures mapped and the sector-specific case for each candidate market set out.

Typically commissioned by a chief operating or supply-chain officer, a corporate strategy team, or an investor underwriting a manufacturing footprint across Asia and the Americas.

05

Sector & Competitive Intelligence

How is the sector actually structured — who competes, who earns, and what lets a newcomer in?

What it answers

How a sector in one or several emerging markets is actually structured: concentration, the competitors and their economics, growth drivers and their durability, pricing, margins and capital intensity, the regulatory frame and its direction, and where a new entrant or an investor could credibly position.

The analytical work

Company filings, regulatory data and official statistics assembled into one sector picture. Competitor benchmarking on disclosed numbers. Growth-driver analysis with the assumptions labelled. Regulatory review. Positioning options tested against the incumbents’ likely response.

Possible outputs

  • Research report
  • Strategy deck
  • Data appendix
  • Briefing call

Where it lands

A sector view an investment or strategy team can act on: where the returns are made, who makes them, and what would have to be true for a newcomer to share in them.

Typically commissioned by a private equity or growth investor ahead of a sector thesis, a corporate weighing a category, or an asset manager building a sector view across markets.

06

Policy & Regulatory Impact

A policy has changed, or is about to. What does it do to your position, your plan and the alternatives?

What it answers

The consequences of a specific policy or regulatory change — a budget, a central-bank turn, a tariff, an ownership rule, a tax reform, an industrial-policy programme, a trade agreement — for a stated exposure or plan, traced through channels rather than headlines, and set against how the same change reads for the alternative markets.

The analytical work

Read the primary text and the official data around it. Establish the transmission channels: rates, currency, credit, trade, costs, demand, ownership. Quantify where the data allows and say where it does not. Compare with precedent in the same market and in peers. State the conditions that would change the conclusion.

Possible outputs

  • Executive memo
  • Briefing call
  • Committee presentation
  • Research report

Where it lands

A clear statement of what has actually changed for you, what has not, and what to watch — before the position or the plan is adjusted.

Typically commissioned by an investment committee, a portfolio manager carrying the exposure, or a corporate affairs and strategy team with an operating footprint in the market.

07

Strategic Scenario Analysis

If rates, currencies, geopolitics, trade, regulation or demand move, what happens to the decision?

What it answers

Structured scenarios around the variables that matter for a specific portfolio, plan or market — rates, currencies, geopolitics, trade, regulation, demand — traced through to positioning or strategy across the relevant markets, with the assumptions stated plainly enough to be argued with and the signposts that would show which scenario is arriving.

The analytical work

Identify the variables the decision is actually sensitive to. Build a small number of internally consistent scenarios rather than a fan of everything. Trace each through the markets’ external accounts, fiscal positions, credit systems and demand structures. Quantify the exposure where the data allows, and name the early indicators for each path.

Possible outputs

  • Research report
  • Economic model
  • Strategy deck
  • Live workshop

Where it lands

A decision stress-tested before it is made, with the exposures ranked by market and the triggers for revisiting it agreed in advance.

Typically commissioned by a chief investment officer or investment committee, a family office, or a board and strategy team preparing a plan across several emerging markets.

Decision support for committees and boards

Any of the seven can be delivered into the room.

Where a major emerging-market decision is going to a committee or a board, the engagement can be structured as decision support around it: the research, the presentation, the briefing and the analytical support the decision needs, timed to the meeting. That includes live presentation to an investment committee, a strategy team or a board by the analyst who did the work, with the questions taken in the room and follow-up discussion afterwards while the decision is being made.

The point is not a slide pack. It is that the people deciding can question the evidence directly, hear where the judgement sits and where the data runs out, and take the decision on a basis they have tested themselves.

Possible delivery formats

  • 01Research report — the full argument, with sources dated and the data shown
  • 02Executive memo — the answer, written for the committee pack
  • 03Strategy deck or committee presentation — built to be presented, or forwarded
  • 04Economic or financial model — with assumptions your side can change
  • 05Data appendix or dashboard — every series sourced, dated and rebuildable
  • 06Live presentation to the investment committee, strategy team or board
  • 07Live workshop — the team works the question with the analyst
  • 08Briefing call or video discussion — before the meeting, and after it

The format follows the decision and the client. Scope, delivery format and timing are agreed before the engagement begins.

How an engagement runs

Four steps

Four steps · Scoped in writing

middot; Nothing starts unscoped

  1. 01

    Scoping call

    The decision, the markets it touches and what a useful answer must contain. The question is tested on the call — and if it cannot be answered well, you are told plainly there, before anything is spent.

  2. 02

    Written scope

    The question, the comparison markets, the outputs, the source standard, the exclusions, the delivery format and the timing — agreed in writing before work starts.

  3. 03

    Research and analysis

    Primary and official sources pulled and cleaned in-house, every figure dated, and connected into one argument — judgement labelled as judgement.

  4. 04

    Delivery in the agreed format

    Report, deck, model, briefing or live presentation — whichever the scope named — with a walkthrough of the reasoning and one round of revision.

Independent research, not execution

Consulting here is research applied to a decision. It does not sell or distribute financial products, take custody of assets, execute or arrange transactions, or provide personalised regulated investment advice; transaction, legal and tax execution stay with you and your advisers. The work narrows the uncertainty around a decision. It does not remove it, and it promises no outcome. That independence — no execution, no product, no external capital — is what lets a conclusion be inconvenient.

Who engages consulting

Six kinds of client · One kind of question

  1. 01

    Asset managers and EM allocation teams

    Choosing between markets for a fresh allocation, re-examining country assumptions the model inherited, or testing a scenario before the committee does. Opportunity assessment, market comparison, policy impact and scenario work.

  2. 02

    Family offices and private capital

    Sizing an emerging-market or India commitment against the alternatives, on a basis the principals can question directly rather than take on trust.

  3. 03

    Corporates and strategy teams

    Entry, expansion and China+1 decisions: where a regional plan should anchor, what the market looks like from the ground, and where the supply chain should sit for this sector.

  4. 04

    Private equity and growth investors

    Sector and competitive intelligence ahead of a thesis, market comparison across a regional mandate, and the policy and regulatory reads that change an underwriting case.

  5. 05

    International institutions and professional firms

    Development finance and multilateral teams, law and accounting practices, and consultancies that need a research layer built to source standard for a specific emerging-market or India question inside their own work.

  6. 06

    Boards and committees

    Decision support around a major emerging-market decision: the research, the presentation in the room and the follow-up discussion, delivered by the analyst who did the work.

Where the questions recur rather than arrive once, a retained desk is usually the better shape: the Emerging Markets Desk for continuous comparative research across your markets, the India Desk for continuous specialist depth on the firm’s deepest market.
